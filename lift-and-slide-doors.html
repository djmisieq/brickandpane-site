Choosing a system

Lift & slide, inline slider or bi-fold?

All three can work in a London extension. They differ in panel size, how much frame you see, how well they seal and how they feel to use every day. This is the comparison we talk clients through at Stage 2.

How it works

A half-turn of the handle does the heavy lifting.

  1. Closed and lowered

    The panel sits down on its gaskets. Its own weight compresses them, which is why lift & slide doors seal so well against wind, rain and noise.

  2. Turn the handle 180°

    A gear in the sash lifts the panel a few millimetres onto steel rollers. The gaskets come clear of the frame.

  3. Slide and park

    The panel glides on the rollers with a light push. You can leave it at any position, and a soft-close damper is an option.

Configurations

Six layouts cover almost every opening.

Plan views. Accent lines are sliding panels; grey lines are fixed. We pick the layout from your opening width, the panel weight and where you want the door to park.

LS-2

Two panels, one sliding

The simplest layout and the best value. One panel parks behind the fixed one, so the clear opening is about half the width.

LS-3T

Three panels, three tracks

Two panels stack behind one fixed panel and open two-thirds of the width. Good when one side must stay clear.

LS-4C

Four panels, centre opening

The classic garden elevation: symmetrical, and the two middle panels part to the sides. It keeps panel weights down on wide openings.

LS-6C

Six panels, centre opening

For openings over about 7 m. Four panels slide on three tracks and two-thirds of the width opens.

LS-CNR

Corner, no post

Two sliding panels meet at a corner with no post, so the garden corner opens completely. The structure must carry the corner load instead: we coordinate this with your engineer at Stage 3.

LS-PKT

Pocket

The panels slide into a pocket in the wall, leaving the full width open and no frame in view. This needs a cavity designed for it, so it must be decided by Stage 3.

    Thresholds

    A flush threshold is a floor detail, not a door option.

    Getting inside and outside onto one level is the reason most clients choose lift & slide. It only works if the slab, insulation and drainage are drawn for it.

    • The slab needs a recess of the depth the system needs. We give you that dimension at Stage 3.
    • The thermal line has to run under the frame to avoid a cold bridge, so coordinate it with the insulation and any underfloor heating.
    • Outside, fit a linear drain and lay the paving to fall away. On exposed sites, a low upstand may be the safer choice.
    • For Part M, keep any upstand to 15 mm or less.

    Designing in London

    Four things that decide whether a big slider works.

    Most problems with large sliding doors start long before installation. These are the four we check on every set of drawings. The design guide covers each one in detail.

    01 · Structure

    Beam deflection

    A sliding head tolerates only a few millimetres of movement. Your engineer should design the beam to the system limit, not just to a general span ratio.

    02 · Floor

    Recess and drainage

    A flush threshold needs a formed recess, a continuous thermal line and a drain outside. All three must be fixed before the slab is poured.

    03 · Access

    Getting the glass in

    A 300 kg panel won't go through a Victorian hallway. We plan the route from kerb to opening at survey, and that can mean a crane licence.

    04 · Regulations

    Part L, O, K, Q

    Glazing over 25% of the extension floor area needs SAP. New homes need an overheating check, safety glass and PAS 24 security.

    Questions

    Lift & slide FAQ

    How wide can a lift & slide door be?

    There's no practical limit on overall width; it's a question of how many panels you use. Each panel is limited by weight (typically up to 400 kg) and size (around 3.3 m wide). Wide openings use four or six panels. Use the sizing tool for a first check.

    Is a 300 kg panel hard to open?

    No. Once lifted onto its rollers the panel needs only a light, steady push. The handle's gear does the lifting. For very large panels we offer concealed motorisation.

    Lift & slide or bi-fold for a London extension?

    Bi-folds open nearly the whole width, but you see a frame every metre or so when they're closed, and that's most of the year. Lift & slide gives bigger glass, slimmer sightlines and better sealing, with half to two-thirds of the width opening. See the comparison.

    Can I have a completely flush threshold?

    Yes, if the floor is designed for it: a recess in the slab, a continuous thermal line and drainage outside. On exposed or high sites, a low upstand of 15 mm or less is often the more robust choice and still meets Part M.

    Will it meet Building Regulations?

    For an extension, the door's U-value needs to meet the limiting value in Approved Document L, and the total glazing should stay within 25% of the extension floor area for the simple route. Above that, your energy assessor uses SAP. New dwellings also need PAS 24 security (Part Q), safety glazing (Part K) and an overheating assessment (Part O). We provide the data for each.

    Do I need planning permission?

    Many rear extensions fall under permitted development, but not in conservation areas where Article 4 directions apply, and not for listed buildings. Planning is your architect's call. We can supply elevations and sightline drawings for the application.

    Who makes and installs the doors?

    We make them to order on a branded system and install them with our own team. We don't subcontract fitting. You get one project manager from survey to handover.

    You're a new company. Why should we trust you with a big opening?

    That's a fair question. The answer is in what you can check: certified system data, a full-size sample before you order, written change control, our own installers and a guarantee from a UK company. Read how our founding projects work.
