Slim double glazing or secondary glazing?

In a period house, especially in a conservation area or a listed building, standard double glazing often isn’t an option. The two usual answers are slim double or vacuum glazing in the window itself, and secondary glazing on the inside. Here is how to choose.

The short answer

  • Secondary glazing when the original windows and glass should stay: listed buildings and surviving historic glass. With a wide gap it is also the best answer to traffic noise, and it can be taken out again.
  • Slim double or vacuum glazing when the sashes are being replaced or reglazed anyway and the conservation officer accepts it.
  • In a listed building, either can need listed building consent. Ask the council first.

1. Slim double and vacuum glazing

A slim double glazed unit is thin enough to fit the shallow rebate of a sash, sometimes with a slightly deeper rebate in a new sash. It has a low-emissivity coating and a gas fill. Vacuum glazing is thinner still and usually insulates better. Both make the sash heavier, so the weights or balances have to be matched to the new glass.

On sashes with several panes, conservation officers usually prefer true glazing bars, with a separate unit in each pane, to bars stuck on a single sheet of glass.

2. Secondary glazing

Secondary glazing is an independent slim frame fixed inside the window reveal. It can slide, hinge or lift out, and it leaves the original window and its glass untouched. The gap between the two decides what it does best: a narrower gap for heat, 100 mm or more for noise.

To avoid condensation, the inner window should seal better than the outer one, so warm, moist room air doesn’t reach the cold outer glass. A little ventilation of the gap through the outer window helps.

3. Planning and consent

On an unlisted house, internal secondary glazing doesn’t need planning permission. Replacement sashes of similar appearance are usually permitted development, unless an Article 4 direction removes that right. In a listed building, anything that affects its character needs listed building consent, and that can include reglazing and secondary glazing. Secondary glazing is often the option conservation officers prefer, because it can be removed. See our conservation areas guide.

4. Building Regulations

When you replace whole windows, they have to meet Approved Document L: a U-value of 1.4 W/m²K or better, or Window Energy Rating band B. For historic buildings, building control can accept a different approach where meeting the limit would harm the building’s character. Agree it before the work starts.

Questions

Is vacuum glazing better than slim double glazing?

It is thinner and usually insulates better, so it suits slender sashes. It also usually costs more. Both need the sash weights or balances matched to the new glass.

Does secondary glazing need planning permission?

Not on an unlisted house: it is internal. In a listed building it can need listed building consent, so ask the council’s conservation officer first.

Which is better for traffic noise?

Secondary glazing with a gap of 100 mm or more between the two windows. Slim double glazing helps a little, but the gap does most of the work.

Can I fit slim double glazing in a listed building?

Sometimes, decided case by case. It is more likely to be accepted where the historic glass has already been lost. Where original glass survives, expect to keep it and add secondary glazing instead.
